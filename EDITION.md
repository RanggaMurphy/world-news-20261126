## How to read this page

Start with the date. It is the biggest line on the page after the
masthead, and it is the page's promise: everything below it was
first reported on 26 November 2026. Then read the three features
across the top. Then the grid, left to right, top row then bottom
row. Then the map, which shows where the thirteen pictured stories
happened. Then the briefs, two columns, top to bottom. Then the
footer, which is the audit: the date check and the certainty legend.

The certainty chips are the page's honesty system. CONFIRMED means
two or more outlets agreed. OFFICIAL means the facts come from an
on-record document. REPORTED means one wire or local source carried
it. CLAIM means one party says it happened and nobody independent
has confirmed it. Two stories on the page are claims, and both are
labelled as such in red.

## For the competition reader

The judging notes for this format say the deciding factors are
completeness and readability at small size, not dense micro text.
This page was built to that brief:

- 36 dated stories, above the 29-story floor and inside the 33 to
  38 target band.
- Three tiers, all numbered, with sport in the grid.
- A region strip, a UTC strip, and a numbers strip, each carrying
  real figures from the day.
- Thirteen drawn scenes, one per pictured story, never photographs.
- A filled-silhouette world map with thirteen numbered pins, each
  pin in its country's colour.
- A certainty label on every story and a printed legend.
- A red-bordered date check that names the stories left out for
  belonging to other days.
- Readable at 300 pixels wide: the masthead, the date, the feature
  headlines, and the strips still read.

## Afterword

The day this page records was a strange one. It opened with a
treaty in Canberra and a claim in the Gulf, broke with a fire in
Cairo and a rout in Taiwan, and closed with a comeback on Wall
Street and a siege ending in Cartagena. A tanker, a refinery, a
market, a parade, a treaty, and a rocket all made the same page,
which is what a world in one day looks like when the news refuses
to pick a single theme.

The desk's favourite detail is the smallest one: the Macy's parade,
which has flown for a century without ever being postponed, was
postponed by wind. The biggest number is the bleakest: 500,000 war
dead on Moscow's own count, a figure the desk prints because it was
cited, not because it can verify it. Both belong on the same page.
That is the job.

## Corrections policy in practice

A correction goes here with three things: the old figure, the new
figure, and the source of the correction. The desk does not correct
silently, and it does not correct by editing the page after the
commit. The page is a record of what was known on the day; the
correction is a record of what changed. If a figure moves after
press time, the page stays as it was and the correction stands next
to it, so that a reader can see both the original and the truth.
